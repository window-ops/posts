The Economics of Loneliness
In the traditional worldview, people took for granted that their work was their own pursuit and their friendships were genuine bonds - both spheres belonging privately and personally to each individual. This notion, once considered common sense, now almost seems radical in our contemporary landscape. The reason for this shift can be traced to the deep influence of neoliberal thought, which has reshaped how we view work, friendship, and even the boundaries of our own lives.

The Neoliberal Shift: Redefining Work and Friendship

Neoliberalism, with its market-first philosophy, regards social and personal relations primarily as functions of economic activity. In this vision, the state's purpose is simply to prop up and lubricate market mechanisms. There's rarely a fixed definition for fundamental human experiences - friendship, for example, is reduced to ideas like "friendly competition". But this isn't real friendship; it's the kind of thinking that whispers "better my friend be out of work than me". The market logic seeps into every corner of life, recasting collaboration as rivalry and loyalty as expendable.

The Roots of Modern Loneliness

This detachment did not happen overnight. Its roots lie in the birth of industrial capitalism during the Industrial Revolution. As ways of living and working changed, so too did the social fabric. Thinkers have long predicted that the fragmentation brought by industrial and then neoliberal capitalism would lead to increased isolation. Today, the epidemic of loneliness is not simply a matter of personal failure, nor can it be blamed solely on technology like smartphones. The problem is economic at its core - a product of systems that encourage individualism at the expense of communal ties.

Economics, Not Electronics

We often hear that loneliness is a result of our personal choices or modern technology, but these explanations overlook the bigger picture. Economic determinism tells us that, in a market-oriented world, relationships often become a zero-sum game. Jobs, security, and even social capital are scarce and competitive resources, inevitably setting us against one another, even in our friendships.

There Is Another Way

Despite this, the old wisdom still holds power: we can choose to foster genuine connections. Friendship does not have to be a casualty of economic systems. By reclaiming work and social life from market logic, we make space for authentic relationships to flourish. The answer to loneliness isn't simply "less screen time" - it's solidarity, community, and the conscious refusal to let economics dictate all aspects of human life.
