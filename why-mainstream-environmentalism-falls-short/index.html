Why Mainstream Environmentalism Falls Short
Mainstream, or anthropocentric, environmentalism has long been touted as the solution to the climate crisis. Yet, it has proven to be insufficient and often nothing more than greenwashing. Major Western European parties like Germany's B90/DG, which claim to champion green policies, exemplify this failure. Their approach, rooted in social capitalism with a green veneer, perpetuates the very system driving ecological collapse.

Compromise and Corporate Influence

Green parties often operate in compromise with neoliberal parties. These compromises, while seemingly progressive, allow corporations to continue operating with impunity, exemplified by mechanisms like carbon credits that enable polluters to buy their way out of accountability. This approach reduces environmentalism to a market transaction, failing to address the root causes of ecological destruction.

A broader example of this is the approval of the coal mine expansion in Lützerath, Germany, by the Greens. Despite their environmental rhetoric, the Greens decided to support the expansion of the coal mine, prioritizing short-term economic interests over long-term ecological sustainability. This decision highlights how mainstream environmentalism often succumbs to corporate and political pressures, undermining its own goals.

Contradictions in Policy-Making

Additionally, green parties (Greens/EFA) in the European Union have also shown contradictions in their policy-making. For instance, many green MEPs supported Ursula von der Leyen's European Commission term extension (VDL 2.0), which has been associated with policies like austerity measures and trade agreements, such as EU–Mercosur Association Agreement, that could increase environmental degradation through outsourcing and extensive resource extraction. This vote indicates how green parties may prioritize political alliances and growth over strict environmental principles.

Global Inequalities and Ecological Imperialism

Moreover, mainstream environmentalism fails to address the global inequalities perpetuated by imperialism. The Global North's "green" facade is built on outsourcing labor and materials to the Global South, where exploitation and pollution are rampant. Products are shipped across the globe multiple times, leaving a massive carbon footprint, simply because it’s cheaper for corporations. This is not sustainability, it's ecological imperialism.

An example of this is the production of electric vehicles and renewable energy technologies, which rely heavily on raw materials like cobalt, lithium, and rare earth elements. Much of these resources are extracted from countries like the Democratic Republic of Congo and China under exploitative and environmentally destructive conditions. In the DRC, cobalt mining is often linked to child labor, unsafe working conditions, and severe environmental degradation. Meanwhile, China dominates the processing and manufacturing of these materials, often at the cost of local ecosystems and communities. Yet, the Global North markets electric cars and green technology as symbols of sustainability, conveniently ignoring the ecological and human toll in the Global South.

The Unsustainability of Capitalism

The core issue lies in the obsession with infinite growth under capitalism. This model is inherently unsustainable, yet mainstream environmentalism refuses to challenge it. Instead, it offers half-measures: replacing nuclear energy with coal plants, for instance, under the guise of sustainability. Such moves are not green, they are regressive, prioritizing short-term political gains over long-term ecological survival.

The Unsustainability of Capitalism

The solution lies in a better framework social ecology, a framework championed by the red-greens that links environmentalism to social justice, related to eco-socialism, degrowth and indigenous ecological knowledge in some way or another. It acknowledges that the ecological crisis cannot be separated from the exploitative structures of capitalism. Social ecology calls for systemic change: decentralizing power, democratizing production, and prioritizing local, sustainable economies, prioritizing both the environment and well-being of the people at the same time.

To truly tackle climate change, we must abandon the illusion that capitalism can be greened. The climate crisis demands more than half-measures. It’s time to embrace systemic change, and prioritize both people and the planet at the same time.
