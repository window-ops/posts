Why STEM Alone Isn't Enough
The belief that STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics) expertise alone grants authority over social issues is a dangerous myth. While technical knowledge is essential for tackling complex problems like climate change or automation, it cannot operate in isolation. Social challenges are inherently political and embedded in histories, power structures, and systemic inequalities - realities best understood through humanities and social sciences.

The Limits of Technocracy
  • Climate Change Is Not Just an Engineering Problem: Kurzgesagt's popular video on climate change (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LxgMdjyw8uw) frames the crisis as a puzzle solvable through innovations such as carbon capture, electric vehicles and other kinds of innovations. But this technocratic narrative overlooks the roots of environmental degradation in colonialism, capitalism, and unequal resource distribution. A critic (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uCuy1DaQzWI) pointed out that such framing depoliticizes the crisis, erasing the voices of communities disproportionately harmed by ecological collapse and simply not offering an actual solution to climate change.
  • The Korean Fertility Crisis: Kurzgesagt's video on South Korea's declining birthrate (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ufmu1WD2TSk) acknowledges economic pressures and the burdens placed on women. Yet, it presents the crisis primarily through a GDP-centric lens, missing deeper questions: Why have children in an ultra-capitalist system that fragments communities and equates human worth with productivity? While somewhat more nuanced than their climate video, this analysis still reflects a neoliberal worldview that treats systemic oppression as inescapable rather than a problem to be actively dismantled.
STEM Cannot Replace Critical Theory

Consider the example of automation and artificial intelligence (AI). These technologies hold the promise of freeing workers from monotonous labor. Yet without robust labor rights frameworks, automation often exacerbates job insecurity and inequality. Similarly, while many STEM advocates acknowledge algorithmic bias, their discussions frequently emphasize civility and technical fixes over addressing the lived concerns of marginalized communities. Algorithmic bias is not simply a problem of "improving code" - it reflects and reinforces social power imbalances that demand decolonial and anti-racist analysis.

Humanities and social sciences equip us to ask crucial questions: Who benefits from a given technology? Who is harmed? What power structures are reinforced or challenged? These are questions STEM alone cannot answer.

The Neoliberal Trap

The push for universal technical training ("everyone should code"), often portrayed as empowering, frequently serves corporate interests by producing skilled but politically disengaged workers. This "neoliberal trap" frames social challenges as individual or market failures rather than systemic issues needing collective action. True solutions demand coalition-building with marginalized communities and scholars of race, gender, and class - not only engineers.

Reclaiming Authority

Social issues rightfully belong to those most affected by them: the oppressed, the organized, and the scholars who center their struggles. Ceding authority to STEM "saviors" risks perpetuating the very systems we seek to dismantle. Instead, we must listen to critical theorists and activists alongside scientists and coders.

Final Thought: Expertise in physics does not qualify someone to redesign society any more than a sociologist should build a nuclear reactor. Interdisciplinary humility - recognizing the limits and strengths of all fields - is non-negotiable for meaningful social change.
