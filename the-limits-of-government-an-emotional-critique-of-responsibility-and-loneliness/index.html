The Limits of Government: An Emotional Critique of Responsibility and Loneliness

I understand the role of a leftist government. It must prioritize material security, ensuring economic democracy, protecting minorities, and improving the lives of the working class. A government operates within ideological frameworks, guided by data and political realities. It is not designed to tend to our loneliest moments or personal traumas. If I feel lonely, it is not just personal grief but a symptom of austerity, capitalism, and neoliberalism. Only by transitioning to democratic socialism or a similar economy can we begin to dismantle these causes.

Yet, if after all those grand systemic changes I still feel lonely, what then?

I know this line of questioning is not rational. My emotions aren’t anyone’s political responsibility but my own. The government, psychologists and social programs cannot manufacture the friendships I long for, nor guarantee steady companionship. They do not owe me their care or time. Their duty is social engineering, systemic reform, awareness, and expansion of rights.

I am lonely right now. That loneliness may threaten my health and my spirit. Still, I must face the uncomfortable truth again: no institution exists to fix my loneliness for me. In combatting loneliness, institutions bear the burden of large-scale prevention, such as stopping pandemics, halting urban sprawl, ensuring universal healthcare.

Loneliness is said to be primarily a problem of the global north, less understood or reported in the global south. Our government, by design, must respect, assist, and uplift those more globally vulnerable regions. Helping the global south such as by combating colonial legacies, neocolonial economic exploitation, and political marginalization, must take precedence over my personal feelings.

Universally proclaimed human rights affirm everyone’s freedom, dignity, and equality. Alongside these come social rights, including the sometimes painful reality that the world is not obliged to cure, console, or even notice my hurt. The personal is political, but only up to a point. When one steps beyond the struggles of marginalized groups, personal hardships remain just that: personal.

Our governments must focus on the solid, collective changes that reshape society, such as ending austerity, building equality, nurturing global solidarity. Because if we lose sight of that, then the personal becomes political in an empty sense, and the essential, structural justice we seek slips beyond reach.
