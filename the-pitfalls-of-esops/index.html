The Pitfalls of ESOPs

Employee Stock Ownership Plans (ESOPs) are often heralded as a way to democratize workplace ownership, boost worker morale, and reduce income inequality. By giving employees a stake in the companies they work for, ESOPs seem to promise a more equitable and participatory economic model. However, a deeper look, drawing both from the simulation game Frostpunk 2 and broader socio-economic theory, reveals critical challenges and contradictions in relying on ESOPs as a pathway to democratic socialism.

The "Auto-Foremaning" Problem: Conflicting Structures of Control

In Frostpunk 2, a fascinating dynamic occurs when two opposing laws are active simultaneously: Labour Oversight, which enforces hierarchy through appointed foremen, and Abolished Management, which seeks to flatten hierarchies by removing managerial roles entirely. This clash leads to what can be called "auto-foremaning", where workers paradoxically end up both undermining and self-enforcing hierarchical roles at once, creating friction, confusion, and inefficiency.

This phenomenon offers a metaphor for real-world attempts to implement ESOPs or worker cooperatives without addressing underlying capitalist structures. Simply abolishing formal management without dismantling the capitalist framework (like capital ownership, profit motives, and competition) leads to internal conflicts over control, power struggles among workers, and a tendency for informal hierarchies to re-emerge.

ESOPs Without Structural Change: A Recipe for Conflict and Stagnation

While ESOPs give workers shares, they often leave the fundamental capitalist incentives intact:

  • Capital remains concentrated in the form of shares, often distributed unevenly.
  • Profit motive persists, pressuring worker-owners to maximize returns.
  • Market competition continues, requiring companies to prioritize growth and efficiency over worker well-being or democratic values.

In such a context, workers may become locked in contradictory roles: owners who must compete and cut costs, yet also workers who seek fair treatment and meaningful input. This tension can stifle cooperation and democratic decision-making.

Moreover, without a supportive system that explicitly fosters collective governance and prioritizes social goals, ESOPs risk reinforcing traditional hierarchies in disguise—where informal managers or "foremen" emerge to mediate conflicts or steer decisions, replicating what Frostpunk 2 dramatizes as "auto-foremaning".

The Decline of the United Airlines ESOP

A pertinent example illustrating the problems raised here is the United Airlines ESOP established in the 1990s. Initially praised for empowering employees by giving them significant ownership stakes, the ESOP failed to create a truly democratic or equitable workplace. The ESOP structure did not eliminate traditional management hierarchies or the profit-driven culture; instead, workers became owners on paper but remained constrained by top-down decision-making and intense market pressures.

Ultimately, during the financial crises of the early 2000s, the ESOP collapsed. Employees suffered significant losses as the company declared bankruptcy, and management reasserted control, undermining the promised empowerment. This case highlights how ESOPs, when implemented without addressing systemic capitalist dynamics and management structures, can lead to worker disenchantment, conflict, and failure, echoing the "auto-foremaning" problem of conflicting authority and interests.

Mondragon: A Model That Avoids "Auto-Foremaning"

Contrasting with many ESOPs, the Mondragon Corporation in Spain exemplifies a true worker-owned cooperative that effectively avoids the "auto-foremaning" problem. Mondragon's democratic governance structure empowers workers directly through participatory decision-making bodies and cooperative principles. There is no ambiguous middle ground where workers both reject and enforce hierarchical control; instead, hierarchy is minimized, and management roles are accountable to the cooperative membership as a whole. Mondragon's success demonstrates that with intentional design and a collective ethos, worker ownership can function smoothly without slipping back into disguised forms of management or internal conflict.

Toward a More Holistic Democratic Economic Model

The critique here is not that ESOPs are inherently bad, but that they are insufficient as standalone reforms. To move beyond neoliberal capitalism toward democratic socialism, we need systemic transformation:

  • Abolish or radically reform management structures in ways that also change ownership and governance.
  • Remove the profit motive as a central organizing principle, shifting focus from growth to sustainability, equity, and social welfare.
  • Design institutions and legal frameworks that support transparent, collective decision-making instead of hierarchical control.
  • Encourage economic models that emphasize cooperative planning, solidarity, and public purpose.

Conclusion: ESOPs Are Only One Piece of the Puzzle

ESOPs can be a valuable tool for increasing worker stake and engagement, but on their own, they risk replicating the contradictions of capitalist production. The "auto-foremaning" problem from Frostpunk 2 illustrates how unresolved tensions between top-down management and bottom-up equality can create inefficiencies and conflicts. Without a broader shift away from profit-driven capitalism and toward democratic planning, ESOPs alone cannot realize the vision of a truly democratic economy.

In short: democratizing ownership requires democratizing the system itself, not just redistributing shares. Only then can we build workplaces and societies that function collaboratively, equitably, and sustainably.
