Rethinking the Non-Inclusive View of Social Skills

Social skills are often treated as a fixed set of mechanical behaviors to be taught and mastered, especially in educational settings. This narrow approach overlooks the rich, dynamic, and context-dependent nature of human interaction, particularly among children and teenagers. More than simply non-inclusive or reductionist, the dominant framework for teaching social skills is deeply entangled with histories of extractive capitalism, colonialism, and globalization. This article critiques the prevailing methods through educational, political-economic, and cultural lenses, arguing for a profound decolonization and de-globalization of how social skills are understood and taught.

The Limits of "Social Mechanics"

Today's social skills education emphasizes explicit, observable behaviors ("social mechanics"), such as making eye contact, initiating conversations, and interpreting simple cues. Common teaching methods include:

  • Role-playing scripted interactions.
  • Checklists and social stories breaking down social exchanges into fixed steps.
  • Reward systems reinforcing "appropriate" behaviors.

These approaches reverse-engineer naturally learned social behaviors by isolating discrete "skills" from their lived contexts and presenting them as risk-averse, replicable formulas. While aiming to reduce errors or awkwardness, this rigidity stifles authentic growth by discouraging experimentation, spontaneity, and emotional resilience.

Social Skills as Commodified Resources

This extraction of social behaviors mirrors the logic of extractive capitalism, an economic system that strips living, complex resources from environments and communities to commodify them. In social skills education, authentic relational experiences and cultural practices are reduced to isolated, standardized behaviors, often without their original meanings. This commodification prioritizes control, measurement, and replication over the nuanced, relational richness of human connection.

Colonialism and the Cultural Colonization of Social Skills

When mechanized Western social norms are imposed on collectivist or Indigenous cultures, this process becomes a form of cultural colonization. For example:

  • Educational systems often enforce Western individualist norms, imposing a social template alien to many non-Western communities.
  • This echoes assimilation practices in Indigenous boarding schools across North America, where children were forced to abandon native languages, behaviors, and social customs.
  • Mastering these packaged Western social skills served to erase Indigenous identities and communal social practices.

Thus, mechanical social skills teaching is not culturally neutral but a tool of colonial power that transforms diverse social worlds into Western replicas.

Globalization and the Westernization of Social Norms

Neoliberal globalization and the internet accelerate the spread of Western-centric social skills pedagogy worldwide. For instance:

  • Websites like verywellmind.com (this specific article, among some others with similar themes) and socialself.com offer guides on Western conversational styles.
  • Social self-help content predominantly reflects Western individualistic values, marginalizing non-Western approaches found in collectivist societies.

This digital globalization:

  • Homogenizes diverse cultural social norms under Western hegemonies.
  • Fuels markets for private and neoliberal public schools aiming to produce conforming workers matching capitalist ideals.
  • Erases alternative epistemologies, limiting the user's ability to learn social skills aligned with their own cultural contexts.

Differential Impact: Why This Education Is Often Redundant for WEIRD Populations

It is important to recognize that the critiques of mechanical social skills education primarily target its imposition on non-Western, Indigenous, and marginalized communities. For many white children in the US or Western Europe, often described as WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic) populations, these social skills and behavioral norms are largely already embedded through socialization from early childhood. These populations grow up immersed in the very cultural paradigms that the standardized social skills curricula promote. As a result:

  • Repetition of Familiar Norms: The teaching of scripted behaviors such as eye contact, conversational initiation, and individualistic frameworks often reiterates what WEIRD children have already internalized from their family, school, and media environments.
  • Reduced Cultural Dislocation: Unlike Indigenous or collectivist cultural groups, children from these backgrounds typically do not experience the alienation or cultural erasure that standardized Western social skills education can inflict.
  • Normalization vs. Colonization: While the methods may be limiting or reductionist for anyone, their function for WEIRD populations tends to reinforce existing social norms and expectations, rather than act as a mechanism of cultural imposition or colonial control.

Author's Positionality and Context

The author identifies as part of the EEDLT demographic - Eastern, Educated, Developing, Lower (Middle) Class, and Transitionary - specifically rooted in Romania, a country not typically classified as WEIRD. This positionality informs the critique presented here, which primarily interrogates social skills education trends and cultural hegemonies observed in Western societies and most prominently portrayed on the internet.

Romania's ongoing social and political developments illustrate the global reach of these Western influences. For example, recent political shifts, such as the ascent of the prime minister Bolojan in 2025, whose governance style echoes elements seen during the 2025 Trump administration in the United States, reflect a growing adoption of American cultural and political patterns. This copying amplifies the infiltration of Western-centric models within Romanian society, making the critique relevant not only for non-Western Indigenous groups but also for populations navigating post-communist transitions grappling with imported Western norms.

Toward Decolonizing and De-Globalizing Social Skills

Meaningful reform requires more than tweaking mechanical teaching methods. Because social skills education is embedded in extractive capitalism and colonial legacies, we must:

  • Center diverse cultural paradigms of social interaction, validating collectivist, Indigenous, and alternative worldviews.
  • Prioritize lived experiences and relational complexity over standardized behaviors and checklists.
  • Foster adaptive, contextual social learning that embraces risk, awkwardness, and emotional nuance.
  • Empower communities to define their own social norms and teaching methods, resisting imposed standards.
  • Acknowledge and dismantle colonial and neoliberal histories that shaped current social skills education, including assimilation policies and cultural homogenization.
