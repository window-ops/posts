Another Social Contract That We Owe Each Other

Minouche Shafik's What We Owe Each Other marks an important step toward reimagining the social contract in our contemporary world. However, while her ideas are important, we must push further and rethink the very foundations of this contract to address systemic challenges that go beyond individual obligations. The urgency of climate change, economic inequality, and global injustice demands a more robust social contract - one that redefines ownership, responsibility, and equity on a global scale.

Public Control Over Renewables: Breaking the Profit Barrier

One of the most pressing issues is how we finance and implement the energy transition. Renewable energy, though cheaper and cleaner, is unattractive to private investors because it offers low returns and high risk compared to fossil fuels or even traditional bank deposits. This paradox means private capital will likely continue to back fossil fuels that generate higher profits at the expense of the planet.

To overcome this, renewable energy investment must be predominantly public. States and public cooperatives should be the main drivers of renewable infrastructure. Public-private partnerships might seem like a compromise, but they often weaken the social purpose by prioritizing profit motives. Private companies, bound to maximize shareholder value, do not inherently "owe" society anything beyond legal compliance. Therefore, the social contract demands that key strategic sectors like energy transition remain under democratic control with clear social mandates.

Dismantling Capitalism: Profit Motive vs. Social Contract

The current capitalist framework, driven by profit and competition, systematically undermines the idea of a social contract based on mutual responsibility and fairness. It is almost paradoxical to enforce a new social contract under the same logic that corrodes social bonds, namely, relentless profit-seeking and wealth concentration.

A transformative social contract requires dismantling capitalism as we know it, or at the very least, radically reforming its core principles. This might mean exploring models that prioritize human well-being and ecological sustainability over private accumulation, such as democratic socialism, cooperativism, or eco-socialism. By relocating the purpose of economic activity from private gain to public good, the social contract becomes a lived reality rather than a theoretical ideal.

Global Justice: Ending Colonial Legacies and Imperialism

Social contracts cannot be confined within national borders, especially when global inequalities are stark and persistent. The Global North enjoys high standards of living built on historical exploitation of the Global South through colonialism and imperialism, which Lenin famously identified as the highest stage of capitalism. This legacy continues to manifest through unfair trade, debt burdens, and resource extraction.

A true social contract must be global, ensuring that the Global South attains comparable living standards, social protections, and environmental justice. Ending neo-colonial practices and imperialist policies is imperative for this. The social contract should enshrine global solidarity, reparative justice, and the dismantling of exploitative global economic structures.

Beyond Universal Basic Income: Guaranteeing Universal Basic Needs

While universal basic income (UBI) has gained popularity as a potential solution to economic insecurity, it risks being a minimalist safety net rather than a full guarantee of well-being. The social contract should guarantee universal basic needs, a comprehensive package including housing, healthcare, education, food security, and clean water, provided unconditionally and free of financial anxiety.

This approach recognizes poverty not as a failure of individuals but as a structural problem to be eradicated through collective responsibility. Finance constraints should never be the limiting factor for basic human dignity.

The Changing Roles of Men, Women, and the Working Class

Social contract debates often focus on women's changing roles, but men's roles are changing, shaped profoundly by evolving material and working conditions. The entire working class faces transformations through automation, precarious labor, and shifting social norms. Gender relations, class dynamics, and labor realities are intertwined and changing simultaneously.

Movements like "HeForShe" highlight how gender justice requires recognizing that men also need to adapt to new expectations and freedoms within these shifting structures. A strengthened social contract must encompass all genders and classes, fostering solidarity and shared responsibility for social progress.

Technology: Double-Edged Sword Reinforcing Inequality

Technology is a powerful force shaping modern life, as Shafik emphasizes. Yet, technology does not inherently break down inequality; rather, under capitalism, it often reinforces existing disparities. Algorithmic bias, digital divides, and the commodification of data benefit the wealthy and corporate powers while marginalizing the vulnerable.

For technology to serve the social contract rather than undermine it, it must be democratized and oriented toward inclusivity, equity, and sustainability. Public oversight and community-driven innovation are key to this transformation.

Conclusion

Building a strengthened social contract requires bold and systemic thinking. It means reclaiming public control over essential resources, dismantling exploitative capitalist structures, achieving global justice, guaranteeing universal basic needs, reimagining gender and class roles, and democratizing technology. This is not merely an idealistic vision, it is an urgent necessity for a fair, resilient, and sustainable future. Our collective survival and flourishing depend on it. We owe each other nothing less.
