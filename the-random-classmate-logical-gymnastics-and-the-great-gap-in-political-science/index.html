The Random Classmate, Logical Gymnastics, and the Great Gap in Political Science

Imagine you're a 16-year-old random classmate in 9th grade. You don't have any intense, deep interests like philosophy, politics, or climate science. You have lots of friends, laugh often, and live your life in the moment. Now, contrast that with the "counterpart", a lone person who is deeply interested in political science, debates policy, and resonates with basically no one.

In political science, this counterpart is the model thinker. But here's where our logical gymnastics begin: for fun, for irony, and for truth, we're going to declare this counterpart unworthy on social grounds, even though there's absolutely no logical basis for it. We'll exclude them, mock them, and overgeneralize wildly about the disinterested majority. We do this because, as teenagers (and sometimes as adults), we love social exclusion, even when we know it's harmful and academically condemned.

Logical Error #1: Ad Hominem Exclusion

We start by attacking the social worth of the counterpart. "They're lonely, disliked, and boring", forgetting this says absolutely nothing about the quality or truth of their political insights. This is an ad hominem fallacy, where instead of debating ideas, we dismiss entire perspectives based on social popularity.

Logical Error #2: Hasty Generalization

Next, we assume that because we feel disconnected from classmates who are politically engaged, everyone feels the same. "All youths are uninterested except the tiny, lonely minority". It neatly explains why mass political engagement seems impossible, except it unfairly caricatures both groups.

Logical Error #3: False Dilemma

We paint the world as a binary: either you're a popular, superficial random classmate or a socially isolated political nerd, no middle ground allowed. This false dilemma distorts reality, hiding the spectrum of engagement levels and personalities, but it's a familiar mental shortcut to make the social landscape feel simple and knowable.

Logical Error #4: Straw Man

In our argument, the model political scientist is socially inept, unworthy, and annoying. We ignore their real complexity and contributions, making them easy targets for exclusion. By attacking this straw man rather than the nuanced truth, we reveal how easily social exclusion distorts genuine dialogue.

Why Perform These Logical Errors?

Political science plays a crucial role in solving urgent global challenges like climate change by informing effective policies and international cooperation. It is fundamental to preserving democracy, as understanding political systems and citizen engagement helps guard against authoritarianism and erosion of rights. Additionally, political science informs how societies can achieve greater social benefits for all by analyzing and promoting fair governance and social justice.

But the lens through which political science is viewed is often shaped by who actually engages with it, rather than the people that may be affected by it. The youth who may read and reflect on this science tend to be part of the interested minority. Disinterested people, by definition, rarely read or engage with such material, making it difficult to genuinely incorporate their perspectives beyond anecdotal or secondhand impressions unrelated to the research, if any exists at all.

Some perceptions of youth apathy and disengagement are inevitably colored by personal experiences, such as my own as a 16-year-old in school. This article itself emerges from that interested group; were I disinterested, it would not exist.

Concepts like Paulo Freire's Pedagogy of the Oppressed offer promising frameworks to foster critical consciousness, yet even these transformative approaches face widespread apathy and disconnection. Freire's ideas require at least a small initial spark of engagement or willingness to participate, something that is often missing.

Ultimately, this article is qualitative and reflects a moment in time. The world may change, and I may feel differently as I grow older. But for now, these challenges around engagement, understanding, and connection (simplified in the title as the Great Gap) persist, particularly within the specific social and historical context of Romania and the broader Eastern European region.
