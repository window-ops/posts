Loneliness in the 21st Century: A Structural Crisis, Not a Choice

In his Big Think video "The Great Friendship Collapse: Inside The Anti-Social Century", Derek Thompson argues that Americans have become lonelier than ever but are choosing this solitude, with technology, particularly smartphones, rewiring our brains to prefer screens over people. His prescriptions emphasize adopting "amistic sensibilities", filtering technology through preexisting values to regain social connection.

While Thompson's observations about increased time spent alone and the effects of technology are important, his central argument, that loneliness is largely a choice, is deeply flawed and ignores critical structural realities shaping modern social life. Loneliness is neither simply a personal preference nor a technological inevitability. Rather, it is fundamentally an economic and societal product of deep-rooted neoliberal capitalist dynamics that have systematically eroded social infrastructure, community bonds, and economic security for large parts of the population.

The Fallacy of Choice in Loneliness

Thompson's framing implicitly assumes a degree of agency and freedom filtered through individual decisions: people choose to isolate themselves, letting technology become a substitute for real-world relationships. But this overlooks the context in which millions make these "choices".

  • Economic precarity: Neoliberal capitalism has reshaped the labor market towards gig work, precarious contracts, extended working hours, and declining benefits. Working poor and lower-middle-class families often face such intense demands and insecurities that socializing becomes a luxury, not a choice. The fictional lonely bouncer in the dystopian narrative of the game Not Tonight, facing deportation after Brexit, exemplifies how social precarity dwarfs socializing as a priority.
  • Spatial and social dislocation: Suburban sprawl, gentrification, and segregation, built into late capitalist urban designs, have fragmented communities physically and socially. The privatization of leisure spaces and absence of affordable "third places" (cafes, community centers, parks) undermines natural points of social bonding.
  • Cultural and psychological toll of capitalism: Growing inequality and consumer culture encourage competition, individualism, and self-optimization rather than collective care. The legacy of industrial and post-industrial capitalism has commodified time, turning social relations into transactional or promotional acts rather than fulfilling bonds.

Technology as a Symptom, Not the Root Cause

Thompson correctly notes that technology privatizes attention and leisure time. But this effect is symptomatic, not causal.

  • Televisions and smartphones are not inherently isolating; rather, advertising-driven business models extract attention in ways that erode sustained communal engagements.
  • Digital gratification and dopamine hits are coping mechanisms responding to work pressures, housing crises, lack of genuine community institutions and not merely choices made from convenience.
  • The rise of AI companions and digital friendships reflect a desperate search for connection in systems failing to provide the stability and relational depth people need.

The Leftist Critique: Neoliberal Capitalism as a Root Cause

Sociological, economic, and cultural analyses from a broad leftist consensus stress that loneliness is a structural problem. It is a product of:

  • Deregulation and dismantling of welfare states, which remove collective supports that once enabled stable social networks.
  • Market-driven housing policies, which displace and isolate communities.
  • The gig economy, which atomizes workers and erases workplace camaraderie.
  • Precarity-induced mental health crises, which generate social withdrawal.

This perspective suggests systemic remedies rather than individual behavior changes, as lonely people often cannot afford not to prioritize survival over socializing.

Conclusion: Loneliness Is Not a Choice in the Anti-Social Century

To say loneliness is a choice is to misunderstand the lived realities of millions. It ignores the economic, political, and cultural matrix within which this feeling proliferates. While Thompson's proposed "amistic" approach to technology use can be a meaningful starting point for individual reflection, it cannot alone overcome the profound consequences of neoliberal capitalism. Real solutions require collective social and economic transformation that rebuilds community infrastructure, ensures economic security, and fosters sustained human connection.
