The Urban Landscape of Inequality: How Capitalist Design Prioritizes Cars and Profit Over People
Urban design reflects the values and priorities of those in power. In many American cities, the construction of highways through urban areas often served racist agendas. An example racist urban planning is the construction of the Cross Bronx Expressway in New York City, which displaced thousands of minority and low-income residents.

In places like Nassau, the prioritization of tourist infrastructure over of local needs has led to a car-dependent environment that neglects pedestrians and small businesses. The design caters to tourists while ignoring residents, a trend seen across many North American cities where the comfort of white populations takes precedence over equity and justice for marginalized communities.

The prevalence of cars in urban spaces is not only unattractive but harmful to local economies, as it often attracts large chain stores that drain resources from neighborhoods without providing real community benefits. Cars also consume a lot of valuable space that could be used for more community-focused developments and it also impedes emergency services and discourages foot traffic (which is crucial for the survival of various local economic facilities).

Lessons can also be learned from the urban designs of "socialist" regimes (which in practice were either state capitalist and/or didn't prioritize the well-being of its people) in Eastern Europe. The utilitarian architecture from the former USSR was primarily focused on efficiency and productivity, often sacrificing community and aesthetic values.

Today, the trend of green gentrification is threatening to displace residents under the guise of sustainability. Wealthy developers often engage in greenwashing to justify demolishing existing communities for luxury developments that most cannot afford. This accumulation by dispossession treats urban space as a commodity for profit rather than a common good. Recognizing the entrepreneurial city as a vehicle for capitalist exploitation is crucial in advocating for a more just and equitable urban landscape that prioritizes people over profit.
