The Ideological Project of IT (IPIT)
Information Technology (IT) shapes modern society in profound ways - from education and economy to governance and ethics. However, the way IT is taught and promoted often carries with it a deeper ideological framework that goes largely unexamined. To call attention to this, we introduce the term IPIT, the Ideological Project of IT - a shorthand coined here to highlight the market-friendly, center-right vision of IT that dominates much public discourse, especially in IT education for children.

What Is IPIT?

IPIT is the conceptual framework that presents IT primarily as a tool for economic growth, workforce development, and competitive advantage. It promotes the idea that coding and digital literacy inevitably lead to better economic outcomes and that teaching IT skills should focus on preparing individuals - starting in childhood - to participate in the market economy. Within IPIT, ethical concerns related to technology, such as bias in AI or surveillance issues, are acknowledged but often reduced to isolated "problems" rather than linked to larger historical and political forces like colonialism, systemic inequalities, or power concentration.

How IPIT Manifests in Politics: Renew Europe

An example of institutional support for the IPIT framework can be seen in Renew Europe, a centrist-liberal political group in the European Parliament. Renew Europe's platform promotes digital skills and innovation as essential to economic competitiveness in a globalized market. Their policies emphasize investment in digital infrastructure, support for startups, and educational programs designed to prepare future workers for digital economies.

While these policies undeniably advance digital literacy and modernization, they rarely confront how digital transformation may reinforce existing inequalities or demand new regulatory frameworks addressing privacy, algorithmic bias, and corporate power concentration.

How IPIT Shapes Education: The Case of Logiscool

Logiscool, a global coding school founded in Hungary in 2014, exemplifies how IPIT operates in the educational sphere. Their mission is to make programming fun, accessible, and engaging for children worldwide with gamified, modular courses designed for different age groups.

Its approach centers on digital literacy as a critical economic skill, presenting coding as a "language of the future" and an essential tool for creativity, problem-solving, and boosting self-confidence. The curriculum emphasizes smooth transitions from block-based to text-based coding - preparing children for real-world programming challenges.

However, personal experience reveals important limitations in this model that reflect IPIT's market-driven orientation. As a former Logiscool student, I found that while the learning was engaging, it involved largely writing code and placing blocks (which are essentially strings of text in a language called StageScript) repetitively until boredom set in.

Moreover, the platform prevented exporting projects outside the system - meaning that learners do not own the means of production. The code and games created remain locked within the platform, denying students the ability to take their creations "home" and modify them freely.

This lack of ownership illustrates how Logiscool treats coding as an individual skill to be acquired within a closed environment, not as a collective, creative act shared among developers and communities. It highlights one of IPIT's key pitfalls: platform-based IT education often limits learners' autonomy and does not encourage them to see code as social property with ethical significance.

How IPIT Aligns with Corporate Interests: Google

At the corporate level, Google epitomizes the IPIT ideology. As a global tech giant, Google heavily invests in promoting coding education and digital skills, sponsoring initiatives and platforms worldwide that align with the notion of preparing individuals for the knowledge economy.

Google's messaging often highlights innovation, entrepreneurship, and open access to information, creating a compelling narrative of progress through technology. However, Google also faces ongoing criticism for its dominance over digital ecosystems, data privacy concerns, and its role in shaping public discourse and digital infrastructure.

By framing IT education and digital literacy as primarily about economic empowerment and innovation, Google's corporate agenda conforms with the IPIT vision, which prioritizes market growth while marginalizing concerns about power asymmetries embedded in digital technologies.

Why IT Education Should Be More Than Market Preparation

IPIT represents a useful but ultimately narrow interpretation of IT's purpose and potential. Although teaching children programming skills is valuable, a one-dimensional focus on economic outcomes risks producing digital citizens who are technically proficient but politically and ethically uninformed.

IT cannot be apolitical. Consider the example of Palantir, a company whose IT products have been used in military surveillance, harsh immigration enforcement, and government secrecy. These examples illustrate how IT can perpetuate harm and systemic abuses if deployed without accountable, ethical frameworks.

At the same time, IT also enables powerful tools for sustainability modeling, democratic engagement, and social innovation. The challenge is to integrate these complexities into how we understand, teach, and govern IT.

Why IT Education Should Be More Than Market Preparation

Instead of solely preparing children to code for the market, IT education should also cultivate critical thinking about:
  • The broad ethical implications of AI and automation, using critical theory
  • The historical and political contexts of technological development
  • How technology intersects with issues of power, justice, and democracy
  • The importance of digital ownership and collective control
This richer educational framework would empower learners to become not just creators of technology but people who recognize their responsibilities to society.

Conclusion

The Ideological Project of IT (IPIT) captures a dominant but limiting vision of technology as merely an economic tool. While it has helped popularize coding education worldwide and promotes valuable skills, it risks ignoring the political, ethical, and social dimensions that are crucial for shaping a just digital future.

Recognizing IPIT as an ideology allows us to question and expand our understanding of IT's role in society. Only then can we ensure that learning technology is also about learning its power and impact - and using it to build a more equitable world.
