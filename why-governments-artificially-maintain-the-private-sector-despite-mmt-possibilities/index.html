Why Governments Artificially Maintain the Private Sector Despite MMT Possibilities
At its core, Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) posits that sovereign governments issuing their own currency can fund large-scale public programs without triggering the inflation feared under conventional wisdom. Indeed, empirical examples such as Japan - with a debt-to-GDP ratio exceeding 200% yet experiencing deflation rather than inflation - demonstrate that large public expenditures do not inevitably destabilize the economy. Despite these realities, governments, particularly in the United States, continue to underfund or conditionally support vital public initiatives, indirectly propping up the private sector and limiting the expansion of comprehensive social programs. This article explores why governments maintain this status quo and the broader socio-political implications.

The Disconnect Between MMT Theory and Political Reality

- MMT's Promise: Funding Public Goods Without Inflation

MMT argues that governments issuing their own sovereign currency can sustainably finance large-scale public projects - such as universal healthcare or socialized housing - without causing runaway inflation, so long as economic slack exists (that is, unused labor and production capacity). Indeed, by directly investing in infrastructure, education, and healthcare, public spending can raise overall productivity. This enhanced economic capacity can place downward pressure on prices, sometimes resulting in deflationary effects rather than inflation.

- Government Reluctance to Fully Fund Public Initiatives

Despite these promising theoretical and empirical insights:
  • Socialized housing projects, often developed by public or non-profit entities, frequently face chronic underfunding, compelling reliance on private capital or incomplete government subsidies.
  • Universal healthcare remains elusive in many countries (either non-existent or gutted), even though it demonstrably improves health outcomes and can reduce overall costs through economies of scale and preventive care.
  • Public education systems remain tethered to outdated paradigms - such as standardized testing and market-oriented reforms (often linked to the Global Education Reform Movement) - instead of evolving toward holistic, human-centered models.
Framed as fiscal prudence or political compromise, this reluctance instead reveals an underlying choice: to artificially sustain the private sector, which might otherwise struggle to compete on a level playing field.

Artificial Maintenance of the Private Sector: Why Governments Do It

- Preserving the Private Sector as an Economic and Political Necessity

Historically, social democracy sought a balance between robust public services and a dynamic private sector. However, experience shows these balances are fragile. Increasingly, neoliberal policies have eroded welfare provisions in favor of market liberalization. Governments - especially those aligned with conservative or centrist ideologies - fear that fully empowering the public sector would destabilize private markets, resulting in economic disruption and political backlash.

Key reasons why governments prop up the private sector include:
  • Maintaining employment in private enterprises and sustaining consumer markets, which in turn preserve political constituencies supportive of capitalist interests.
  • Prioritizing expenditures such as military spending that sustain defense industries and geopolitical influence over welfare or social programs.
  • Ideological resistance rooted in neoliberal and Reagan-era doctrines, which equate public sector dominance with economic stagnation and loss of individual freedom.
- The Consequences of Artificial Support

The consequence is that governments continually underfund or fragment public initiatives, forcing citizens to rely on private providers for essential services. The private sector benefits from government contracts, subsidies, and the perpetuation of consumer dependence - even when private services are less efficient or equitable than public alternatives.

What Happens If the Government Fully Embraces Public Sector Expansion?

- Public Sector Wins: Efficiency, Fairness, and Democratization

If governments committed fully to publicly funded services, several transformative outcomes could emerge:
  • Inefficient or exploitative private industries would face natural corrections or collapse without artificial government props.
  • Citizens might prefer public services due to their potential for higher quality, increased fairness, and lower costs.
  • The public sector could shed bureaucratic inefficiencies by democratizing decision-making, involving citizens directly and fostering transparency.
  • Such a shift would challenge existing social democratic compromises, which depend on managing a delicate balance between market forces and state intervention.
- The Political and Social Pushback

This transformation would provoke strong resistance from entrenched interests benefiting from the status quo:
  • Capitalist classes and private enterprises would oppose losing market share and influence.
  • Political forces aligned with corporate interests could amplify fears of "government overreach" and "loss of freedom".
  • Far-right and neoliberal factions historically work to dismantle progressive gains in favor of market-driven policies.
Beyond Currency: Direct Resource Mobilization Through Democratic, Computer-Led Planning

While Modern Monetary Theory emphasizes that sovereign currency-issuing governments can finance public programs without inevitable inflation, concerns about currency depreciation and monetary instability persist among some critics. The traditional concept of money - as a medium of exchange, store of value, and unit of account - remains central to economic life and political power structures. But what if this whole monetary framework could be transcended?

Emerging socio-economic visions, such as those inspired by some solarpunk aesthetics and principles, propose a radical alternative: ending currency altogether and directly mobilizing resources without intermediaries. Instead of relying on fiat money and financial markets, these models imagine an economy coordinated through a combination of advanced computer-led planning and participatory democratic governance. Here's how this idea reframes the debate:

- Direct Resource Allocation Without Money

By removing money as a necessary intermediary, governments and communities could allocate resources - labor, materials, energy, services - based on real needs, capacities, and sustainability goals rather than market prices or financial flows. This would reduce speculative financial pressures that sometimes drive currency instability or inflationary spirals.

- Computer-Led, Participatory Economic Planning

Modern advances in data analytics, automation, and artificial intelligence open possibilities for sophisticated planning models that process vast amounts of information about supply, demand, and environmental impacts in real time. When combined with democratic input mechanisms - citizen assemblies, local councils, digital platforms - this approach promises transparent, accountable decision-making that aligns economic activity directly with collective values like equity, ecological balance, and human well-being.

- Challenges and Transitional Considerations

Implementing such a system would not be without challenges: technological feasibility, safeguarding democratic participation, managing complex logistics, and overcoming vested interests in existing monetary and financial institutions all require careful navigation. However, by conceptualizing an economy beyond currency, this model offers a bold pathway to address core concerns underlying fears of currency depreciation and economic instability - ultimately changing how societies organize wealth, work, and welfare.
